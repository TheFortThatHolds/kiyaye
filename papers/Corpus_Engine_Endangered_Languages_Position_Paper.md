THE CORPUS ENGINE AND THE FUTURE OF ENDANGERED LANGUAGES
A Position Paper on AI Training Data Generation and Linguistic Survival

Jimmy Thornburg LLC / The Fort That Holds LLC
January 2026

────────────────────────────────────────

THE QUESTION

We can now generate properly translated training data for AI models in endangered languages.

What does that actually mean?

────────────────────────────────────────

THE CURRENT STATE OF AI AND LANGUAGE

Modern large language models learn from text. The more text, the better the model performs in that language.

English: Trillions of words of training data. AI speaks it fluently.
Spanish: Billions of words. AI speaks it well.
Lakota: Maybe a few million words exist in digital form, total.

The result is predictable. Ask ChatGPT or Claude to write in Lakota and you get:
- Broken grammar
- Invented words
- English syntax with Lakota vocabulary pasted in
- Confident wrongness

The AI isn't stupid. It's starving. There's nothing to learn from.

────────────────────────────────────────

WHAT TRAINING DATA ACTUALLY IS

This is important to understand.

Training data isn't just "text in a language." It's examples of how the language WORKS across every domain of human activity:

- How do you explain a math problem in Lakota?
- How do you write a recipe in Lakota?
- How do you argue a legal point in Lakota?
- How do you write a love letter in Lakota?
- How do you explain code in Lakota?
- How do you tell a joke in Lakota?

For English, the internet provides billions of examples of each. The AI learns the patterns.

For Lakota, most available text is:
- 19th century missionary translations (archaic, often poor quality)
- Educational materials (limited vocabulary, simple grammar)
- Traditional stories and songs (one domain, formal register)
- Linguistic documentation (academic, metalinguistic)

There is almost zero contemporary Lakota text about technology, psychology, business, science, modern daily life, internet culture, or any domain that didn't exist 150 years ago.

The AI can't learn to speak modern Lakota because modern Lakota—in written, digital form—barely exists.

────────────────────────────────────────

WHAT CHANGES WITH GENERATED TRAINING DATA

The ULAS framework combined with AI collaboration allows us to generate:

- Properly structured text in the target language
- Across any domain (technical, creative, casual, formal)
- At scale (thousands of pages, not dozens)
- With consistent quality
- Following established grammatical patterns

We demonstrated this by translating a complex AI training data sample into Lakota, Scottish Gaelic, and Cornish—complete with translated code comments, technical explanations, creative writing, and dialogue in multiple registers.

One document. Three endangered languages. Contemporary technical content.

Now multiply that by everything.

────────────────────────────────────────

WILL A CHATBOT SOMEDAY SPEAK FLUENT LAKOTA?

Honest answer: Maybe. With caveats.

THE OPTIMISTIC CASE:

If we generate enough high-quality training data—millions of words across diverse domains—and if that data is verified by native speakers, then yes. A future AI model trained on that corpus could:

- Hold conversations in Lakota
- Explain technical concepts in Lakota
- Write in appropriate registers (formal, casual, ceremonial)
- Help language learners practice
- Assist in translation
- Generate new content that expands the living corpus further

The AI becomes a tool FOR speakers, not a replacement for them.

THE REALISTIC COMPLICATIONS:

Quality verification is the bottleneck.

We can generate unlimited text. But generated text can contain errors—grammatical mistakes, unnatural phrasing, invented constructions that seem plausible but aren't how speakers actually talk.

For English, errors get caught because millions of people read the output and provide feedback.

For Lakota, the verification pool is tiny.

────────────────────────────────────────

THE VERIFICATION CRISIS

Lakota (Lakȟótiyapi):
- Estimated fluent speakers: 2,000 or fewer
- Most are elderly
- Concentrated in specific communities (Standing Rock, Pine Ridge, Rosebud, Cheyenne River)
- Many learned it as a second language during revitalization efforts

Who can verify that a 50-page technical document in Lakota is correct?

Realistically: A handful of people on Earth.

And they're busy. They're often elderly. They may not have technical backgrounds. They have their own lives, their own language work, their own priorities.

We cannot generate our way out of the fundamental problem: the people who KNOW are dying.

────────────────────────────────────────

THE RACE AGAINST TIME

This is not abstract.

Every year, fluent elders die. The knowledge in their heads—the intuitions about what sounds right, the subtle grammatical patterns no textbook captures, the cultural contexts that determine appropriate usage—goes with them.

A language isn't just vocabulary and grammar rules. It's a living system maintained by a community of speakers. When the community shrinks below a critical mass, the system starts to collapse. Variation gets lost. Complexity simplifies. Contact with dominant languages accelerates interference.

Generated training data is not a substitute for living speakers.

But it might be a bridge.

────────────────────────────────────────

THE BRIDGE STRATEGY

Here's a possible path:

PHASE 1: CAPTURE (Now - 5 years)
- Work intensively with remaining fluent speakers
- Generate training data and have them verify/correct it
- Every correction is itself valuable data about how the language works
- Build verified corpora while verification is still possible
- Document not just what's correct, but WHY (metalinguistic knowledge)

PHASE 2: AMPLIFY (5-15 years)
- Train AI models on verified corpora
- Use those models to generate MORE content
- Have remaining speakers and trained second-language speakers verify
- Create tools that help learners (chatbots, writing assistants, pronunciation guides)
- Lower the barrier to using the language in daily life

PHASE 3: SUSTAIN (15+ years)
- AI tools maintain and expand the corpus continuously
- New generations of speakers learn with AI assistance
- The language becomes usable in modern contexts
- Community decides how to evolve the language going forward

This is not language preservation in the museum sense.
This is language LIFE SUPPORT while the patient recovers.

────────────────────────────────────────

THE ETHICAL DIMENSIONS

We must be honest about what we're doing and not doing.

WHAT WE ARE NOT DOING:
- Replacing speakers
- Claiming authority over the language
- Extracting knowledge without consent or benefit to the community
- "Saving" a language from outside without community involvement
- Creating some pure/frozen version of the language

WHAT WE ARE DOING:
- Building tools that communities can use IF THEY WANT THEM
- Expanding the domains where the language can function
- Lowering barriers to literacy and fluency
- Racing the clock to capture knowledge before it's lost
- Amplifying the work of actual language warriors

The community owns the language. We build infrastructure. They decide what to do with it.

────────────────────────────────────────

THE HARD QUESTIONS

Can AI-generated text ever be "authentic"?

Authenticity is complicated. Is a text "authentic Lakota" if:
- It was written by a fluent speaker? (Yes, obviously)
- It was written by a learner and corrected by a speaker? (Probably yes)
- It was generated by AI and verified by a speaker? (This is the question)
- It was generated by AI trained on speaker-verified data? (Harder)
- It was generated by AI with no verification? (Probably no)

The answer might be: authenticity exists on a spectrum, and verified AI-generated content is more authentic than no content at all.

A language that can only discuss topics from 1880 is a museum exhibit.
A language that can discuss anything is alive.

What if the generated data contains errors that become "canon"?

This is a real risk. If AI models learn from flawed data, they reproduce and amplify the flaws. Future learners might learn incorrect forms that then spread.

Mitigation: Rigorous verification protocols. Version control. Clear labeling of confidence levels. Community oversight. Treating the corpus as a living document that can be corrected.

But yes, we might introduce errors. The question is whether that risk outweighs the certainty of losing the language entirely.

What about sacred or restricted knowledge?

Some knowledge is not meant to be shared outside certain contexts or communities. ULAS protocols explicitly require:
- Community consent for all content
- Respect for restrictions on sacred/ceremonial material
- Community ownership of produced content
- Right to exclude any domain from translation/generation

We generate training data for public, secular, contemporary domains. We do not touch what the community says is off-limits.

────────────────────────────────────────

THE SCALE OF THE OPPORTUNITY

UNESCO estimates 3,500+ languages will die by 2100.

That's one language extinction every 8 days, on average, for the next 75 years.

Current preservation methods cannot keep pace. Documentation projects are expensive, slow, and produce archives rather than living corpora. Revitalization programs work but require massive sustained investment that most communities can't access.

AI-assisted corpus generation changes the economics.

One skilled translator working with AI can produce more verified content in a month than traditional methods produce in a year. The output is immediately usable as training data. The training data improves AI tools that make the next round faster.

This is a flywheel that could scale to dozens or hundreds of languages simultaneously.

Not a silver bullet. A force multiplier.

────────────────────────────────────────

WHAT HAPPENS IF WE SUCCEED

Imagine a future where:

- A Lakota child asks a voice assistant a question and gets an answer in fluent Lakota
- A student learning Cornish practices conversation with an AI tutor at 2am
- A Scottish Gaelic speaker dictates emails in their language and the AI transcribes correctly
- Technical documentation exists in minority languages so speakers don't have to switch to English to learn new skills
- Literature, journalism, and creative work can be produced in languages that currently can't support professional writers

These languages stop being "heritage" and start being "normal."

That's what survival looks like.

────────────────────────────────────────

WHAT HAPPENS IF WE FAIL

The languages die anyway, but we tried.

Or, worse: we generate vast corpora of flawed content that poisons the well, teaching learners incorrect forms that displace authentic knowledge.

The stakes are real. This isn't a tech demo. These are actual languages that actual communities are fighting to keep alive.

We proceed carefully. We verify relentlessly. We defer to communities. We build tools, not empires.

────────────────────────────────────────

CONCLUSION

Can we generate properly translated training data for endangered languages?
Yes. We demonstrated it today.

Will a chatbot someday speak fluent Lakota?
Maybe, if we do the work now while verification is still possible.

Are there enough people left to verify the data?
Barely. That's why this is urgent.

The window is closing. The elders are dying. The knowledge is evaporating.

We have tools that didn't exist five years ago. We have frameworks that can maintain quality. We have the ability to generate at scale.

What we don't have is time.

────────────────────────────────────────

CALL TO ACTION

For language communities:
- Consider whether AI-assisted corpus building could help your revitalization efforts
- Maintain control—you own the language and any content produced
- Identify domains where expanded vocabulary would help speakers use the language daily

For linguists and language workers:
- Engage with AI tools as amplifiers, not replacements
- Help build verification protocols that ensure quality
- Document metalinguistic knowledge, not just surface forms

For technologists:
- Build tools that serve communities, not extract from them
- Design for community ownership and control
- Prioritize verification workflows over raw generation speed

For funders:
- This work is cost-effective relative to traditional methods
- Investment now captures knowledge that will be lost within a decade
- Tools built for one language can be adapted for others

The Corpus Engine is running.
The question is whether we fuel it in time.
